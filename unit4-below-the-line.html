Block 1 · Sales Promotion

The fastest lever in your kit, and the easiest one to break

Nothing moves stock like a promotion. Nothing erodes a price premium like a promotion. Both statements are true, and holding them together is what separates a brand manager from a discount dispenser. Start by separating the two audiences every promotion serves.Belch and Belch, Ch. 16

Consumer vs Trade: two games on one board

Tap each card to flip it. One targets the shopper's heart, the other targets the distributor's margin sheet. Confusing the two is how budgets vanish.

Consumer Promotions

Who it targets: the end shopper, the household, the app user scrolling at 11 pm.

The job: pull the product off the shelf. Create trial, reward loyalty, nudge the basket bigger.

The consumer toolkit

  • Typical tools: price-offs, contests, sampling, loyalty points, festive bundles, cashback, scratch cards.
  • Success metric: trial rate, repeat rate, redemption rate, incremental volume net of subsidised volume.
  • Failure smell: volume spikes during the promo, craters after. You rented sales, you did not build demand.

Trade Promotions

Who it targets: the distributor, the stockist, the modern trade buyer, the kirana counter.

The job: push the product into the channel. Win shelf space, displays, and the retailer's recommendation.

The trade toolkit

  • Typical tools: margin slabs, quantity purchase schemes, display allowances, dealer contests, credit terms, free goods.

Objectives, over-reliance, and integration

Every promotion should have one written objective before a single rupee moves: induce trial, accelerate purchase, load the pantry, defend against a competitive launch, clear seasonal stock, or energise the trade. One objective per promotion. When a promo is asked to do three jobs, it does none and the post-mortem blames the creative.

Kotler's discipline applies here: promotions change behaviour, advertising changes minds. Use the promotion to trigger the act, use the advertising to make the act mean something.Kotler and Keller

Say this in your next review: "Which single behaviour is this promotion buying, and what does that behaviour cost us per unit against its lifetime value?"

Run the simulator above at 10 or more promotions per year and you have seen the mechanism. Frequent price promotions teach three lessons you never intended to teach. The shopper learns the real price is the discounted price. The trade learns to stop ordering at full margin and wait for the scheme. Your own sales team learns that the quarter can always be rescued with one more offer.

The result is a brand that cannot sell at its own MRP. India's cashback wars proved this at billion-dollar scale: when the incentive paused, the behaviour paused with it, because the incentive was the brand.

Say this in your next review: "What percentage of our annual volume was sold on deal, and is that number rising? If it is, we are not running promotions, promotions are running us."

An integrated promotion carries the brand's idea inside the mechanic. Cadbury does not discount chocolate at Diwali; it sells Celebrations, a gifting ritual in a purple box, at a premium. The promotion (festive gift packs) and the advertising (Kuch Meetha Ho Jaaye) tell one story. The mechanic is the message.

Contrast that with a naked 40 percent off. It borrows this quarter's volume from next quarter's price integrity. The integration test is simple: if you deleted the logo from your promotion, would anyone know it was yours? If not, you have rented a coupon, not run a campaign.

Say this in your next review: "Show me the promotion and the ad side by side. If they could belong to two different brands, we redo one of them."

Indian cases: tap each to open the lesson

Cadbury Celebrations

Festive gifting bundles: the premium promotion

Cadbury converted a chocolate bar into a Diwali ritual. The bundle is the promotion, yet the price went up, not down.

By packaging assortments into gift boxes tied to Raksha Bandhan and Diwali, Cadbury created a promotion that adds occasion value instead of subtracting price. The shopper is not buying more chocolate, they are buying a socially safe gift, and they happily pay a premium for the packaging and the ritual.

Boardroom takeaway: the highest form of sales promotion changes the occasion, not the price. Ask what ritual your category owns, or could own.

Big Bazaar

Sabse Sasta Din: the event-ised discount

One day, one promise, national queues. Big Bazaar turned a price-off into an annual festival around Republic Day.

By compressing discounts into a dated, named event, Big Bazaar created urgency and anticipation instead of constant erosion. The scarcity was the strategy: shoppers planned for it, media covered it, and the rest of the year the store could hold price. The risk showed later, when everyday low price positioning diluted the event's specialness.

Boardroom takeaway: a discount with a date is an event; a discount without a date is a habit. Habits you fund, events you own.

Paytm vs PhonePe

The cashback wars: a margin lesson written in crores

India's fintechs bought transactions with cashback. The transactions came. The loyalty did not.

Cashback acquired users at extraordinary speed, but it acquired deal-seekers, not believers. The moment incentives thinned, multi-homing users simply followed the next offer. The wallets that survived did so by building use cases (bills, tickets, credit) that made the app useful at full price. The cashback got the install; only utility kept the habit.

Boardroom takeaway: a promotion can buy behaviour, never belief. Budget for what keeps the customer after the incentive stops, or the incentive becomes the product.

Read the strategy ↓
Tata Neu & Flipkart SuperCoins

Loyalty design: points as an ecosystem glue

NeuCoins and SuperCoins are not discounts. They are switching costs dressed as rewards.

A coin earned on groceries and burned on flights makes the ecosystem, not the single purchase, the unit of loyalty. The design questions are earn rate, burn flexibility, and breakage. Done well, the programme protects full price while still rewarding the heavy user. Done badly, it becomes a deferred discount with an accounting liability attached.

Boardroom takeaway: loyalty currency is only as strong as the places it can be spent. Evaluate a programme by its burn side, not its earn side.

Block 2 · Shareability & Virality

Word of mouth is not luck. It is engineering.

Every brand manager prays for virality. The professionals stop praying and start designing: they build the share into the promotion before the promotion ships. If the forward button is an afterthought, the forwards will be too.

A shareable promotion answers three questions at the design stage, not the launch stage. What emotion makes someone hit forward: surprise, status, humour, usefulness, belonging? What exactly travels: a referral code, a meme template, an unboxing moment, a leaderboard rank? And who carries it: friends, family groups, or micro-creators who treat your promo as content?

If your campaign deck has a "virality" slide with no answers to those three, that slide is a wish, not a plan.

Say this in your next review: "Show me the share unit. What is the exact screenshot, line, or link a customer forwards, and what does the forwarder gain socially by sending it?"

In India, the family WhatsApp group is a media channel with better targeting than any DSP. A promotion forwarded by your cousin carries a trust signal that no pre-roll can buy. Dream11 understood this: the referral is not an ad, it is an invitation into a game your friend is already playing, with both sides rewarded.

Micro-creators are the second engine. Give them a mechanic worth performing (an unboxing, a challenge, a reveal) and they manufacture reach for the price of the product. The brand supplies the stage; the creator supplies the audience.

Say this in your next review: "Which of our budget lines pays people, and which one makes people want to participate for free? Grow the second."

Accidental word of mouth is a compliment. Designed word of mouth is a channel. The difference is structural: designed WOM has a trigger engineered into the product moment (CRED's absurd reward reveal), a frictionless share unit (a screenshot that explains itself), and a seeded network (creators and communities primed to carry it in the first 48 hours).

Measure it like a channel too: K-factor (invites sent times conversion per invite), share rate per redemption, and time-to-second-generation share. What gets measured gets designed.

Say this in your next review: "What is the K-factor of this promotion, and which design choice are we changing to raise it?"

Indian cases: engineered to travel

CRED

Absurdist rewards engineered for screenshots

A curtain rises on a reward so strange you must show someone. That is not creativity for its own sake, it is share-unit design.

CRED's reward reveals and its deliberately absurd ads (Rahul Dravid as the Indiranagar ka Gunda) are built to be clipped, screenshotted, and reposted. The brand accepts looking ridiculous because the meme carries the brand name into feeds its media budget could never afford. The screenshot is the medium.

Boardroom takeaway: ask of every reward and reveal, "would a user screenshot this?" If the answer is no, you paid for a transaction and forfeited the broadcast.

Zomato

Notification humour forwarded on WhatsApp

A push notification is a promotion delivery vehicle. Zomato made it funny enough to forward, converting an owned channel into an earned one.

Zomato's notification and social copy runs on insider humour about Indian food habits. Users screenshot the notification and post it themselves, which means a retention message doubles as acquisition media. The craft lesson: the share unit here is text, the cheapest asset in marketing, elevated by voice.

Dream11

Referral mechanics inside a social game

The referral is not an ad interrupting a friendship. It is an invitation to compete inside one.

Dream11's growth loop rewards both inviter and invitee, and the product itself is social: your team versus your friends' teams in the same match. The promotion (referral bonus) and the product (contest with friends) reinforce each other, so each new user has a built-in reason to recruit the next. The share is the gameplay.

Meme-ability as permanent free media

Decades of jokes about an adhesive. Fevicol built a brand so witty that the internet does its advertising between campaigns.

Block 3 · Public Relations

Earned trust beats bought attention

Advertising is you saying you are good. PR is someone else saying it, in their own words, to an audience that trusts them. That third-party voice is why one page of genuine coverage can outweigh a month of paid impressions, and why it cannot simply be purchased.Belch and Belch, Ch. 17

Paid vs earned: tap each row to expand

Dimension: Advertising (paid) vs PR (earned)
Advertising: you do, to the last comma. You buy the space, you fill it.
PR: the journalist does. You can brief, pitch, and hope, but the byline is theirs. Control traded for credibility.
Advertising: media rates, transparent and invoiced.
PR: no media invoice, but real costs: agency retainers, newsroom-grade content, relationships built over years, and the discipline of actually being newsworthy.
Advertising: discounted by default; everyone knows you paid to say it.
PR: carries the outlet's credibility. A business daily writing about your supply chain innovation does what no self-declared claim can.
Advertising: infinitely, as long as budget lasts. Frequency is a dial.
PR: a story runs once. You cannot re-run yesterday's news, you must create tomorrow's.
Advertising: being ignored. Expensive silence.
PR: being covered badly. The same journalists who amplify your launch will amplify your lapse. Earned media cuts both ways.

Block 4 · Crisis & Brand Values

The first 24 hours, and the 24 years before them

A crisis does not test your crisis plan. It tests everything you communicated before the crisis existed. Remember the creative anchor from Unit II: a brand that has consistently expressed one set of values has something to invoke when the sirens start. A brand that has only ever sold has nothing to say that anyone will believe.

The recovery arc: tap each stage

Silence is a statement, and the internet writes your story while you draft yours. The first move is a fast, human acknowledgement: we know, we care, we are on it. No blame, no legal hedging, no "alleged". Speed beats polish in the first hours because the vacuum is your real enemy.

The holding statement buys time honestly. Its anatomy: what we know, what we do not yet know, what we are doing right now, when we will update you next. It never speculates, never minimises, and always names a next checkpoint. Miss that checkpoint and you have created a second crisis.

Internal alignment is external credibility. One spokesperson, one fact sheet, every employee briefed, because a contradicting quote from a regional manager can undo the CEO's statement. Then action the audience can see: recalls, audits, third-party testing. Deeds are the only press release people fully believe.

Recovery is earned with follow-up proof: published results, independent certification, and a return-to-market story that thanks the customer rather than congratulating the brand. Maggi's comeback did not say "we won". It said "we missed you too". That is a brand drawing from its reservoir, not its legal team.

Indian cases: reservoirs, tested

Maggi, 2015

The definitive reservoir story

Banned nationwide over lead-content allegations, pulled from every shelf, then back to market leadership.

Maggi survived a five-month ban because thirty years of "2-minute noodles" and mother-child storytelling had built an emotional reservoir money cannot rush. The comeback campaign, We Miss You Too, spoke to the relationship, not the lab report, while retesting and court proceedings handled the facts. Consumers wanted Maggi to be safe, and that wanting was the asset.

Boardroom takeaway: the ban tested the product; the comeback drew on the brand. Fill the tank before you need it, because you cannot fill it during the fire.

Read the strategy ↓
Cadbury, 2003

The worm crisis and the trust rebuild

Worms in Dairy Milk packets, weeks before Diwali. Sales collapsed. The response became a textbook.

Cadbury moved on two fronts: operations (new double-sealed, foil-wrapped packaging, plant audits, media walk-throughs) and communication (Amitabh Bachchan, a voice India trusted, personally vouching for the process). Note the sequence: fix first, then front the most credible spokesperson available, then let the festive season ritual do the healing. Project Vishwas was aimed at the trade as much as the consumer.

Boardroom takeaway: borrowed credibility works only when backed by visible correction. Bachchan could vouch because the packaging had actually changed.

The contrast case

No reservoir, full price paid

Byju's: a brand that spent big on visibility, and found visibility is not the same as goodwill.

At its peak, Byju's bought cricket jerseys, World Cup sponsorships, and superstar endorsers. But its everyday communication was acquisition-led: sales calls, fee pressure, aggressive conversion. When financial and governance troubles hit, there was no reservoir of felt values to draw on; parents' accumulated frustration surfaced instead, and coverage turned unsparing. Reach had been rented; trust had never been banked.

Boardroom takeaway: sponsorship visibility without value-consistent behaviour builds a tall building with no foundation. Audit what your daily operations are depositing, because in crisis, that is the balance you draw on.

Block 5 · Experiential & Sponsorship

Memory-making, not logo-placing

A logo on a jersey buys visibility. An experience in someone's hands builds memory, and memory is what gets retrieved at the shelf. Evaluate every sponsorship and activation on what it makes people feel and do, not on how many eyeballs allegedly grazed past your logo.

Indian case cards: what the cheque really buys

IPL · Dream11 & Tata

Title sponsorship beyond eyeballs

What do Tata and Dream11 actually purchase with an IPL cheque? Not impressions. Permission structures.

For Dream11, the IPL is category education at national scale: every match normalises fantasy sports and feeds the app's core loop the same evening. For Tata, the title is an association transfer play, the nation's biggest stage stamped with the group's name, reinforcing scale and trust across dozens of businesses. Same property, different strategic jobs. The activation, in-app contests and broadcast integration, is where the value gets banked.

Boardroom takeaway: a property has no inherent value; the fit between its meaning and your strategic job creates the value. Name the job before you negotiate the price.

Read the strategy ↓
Festive activations

Coca-Cola and Cadbury at Diwali, Kingfisher at the festival stage

Festivals are India's native experiential platform: emotion pre-loaded, gathering guaranteed.

Coca-Cola and Cadbury build Diwali activations around gifting and reunion because the festival supplies the emotion; the brand only has to be present inside the ritual. Music-festival activations, the Sunburn-style beer garden or the branded stage, work the same way for youth brands: the memory of the night transfers to the logo that hosted it. Kumbh-scale rural activations show the other extreme, where sampling, utility (water, rest stops), and sheer service build goodwill among crores of pilgrims.

Boardroom takeaway: borrow an occasion's emotion honestly, add real utility or delight, and the memory files itself under your brand.

Read the strategy ↓
Mall & campus

Small stages, sharp lessons

A mall kiosk or a college fest stall is sponsorship on a trainee budget, and the fundamentals are identical.

The kiosk that only distributes pamphlets is logo-placing. The kiosk with a game, a sample, a photo moment, or a skill challenge is memory-making. College activations add an amplifier: students post everything, so a well-designed stall earns its own social reach. Measure footfall to interaction to takeaway conversion, not just footfall.

Boardroom takeaway: if the visitor leaves with nothing to feel, do, or show, the activation was a banner with staffing costs.

Personal selling

The showroom must repeat the ad's promise

Experiential marketing's forgotten sibling: the human at the counter is your last medium.

Tanishq advertises trust and purity, and backs it in-store with the karatmeter, a machine that tests your old gold in front of you. The ad's promise and the showroom's behaviour are one system. Now imagine a luxury car ad promising serenity, delivered by a pushy salesman discounting on the first visit. The last three feet of communication undid the first thousand kilometres of it.

Boardroom takeaway: brief the sales floor like you brief the agency. The showroom script is media; audit it like media.

Block 6 · Regulation & Ethics

The fine print is your signature

Every promotion you approve carries your professional name on it, whether or not it carries your legal one. India's consumer protection framework and the ASCI Code are not obstacles to clever marketing. They are the minimum standard of the promise-keeping business you are in.Consumer Protection Act 2019ASCI Code

Full disclosure

Material conditions of an offer must be stated upfront, not buried. Price, duration, eligibility, and the real odds of a prize. Under the Consumer Protection Act 2019, a misleading advertisement can draw penalties for the manufacturer, the agency, and the endorser. "Conditions apply" in six-point grey is not disclosure, it is documentation of intent.

The conditions-apply abuse

The classic patterns: "up to 80% off" where one dusty SKU carries the 80, free offers with undisclosed shipping loaded, exchange bonuses that quietly require accessories purchases. Each is legal-looking and trust-corroding. The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) now issues guidelines and penalties targeting exactly these dark patterns.

Contests and lottery legality

A game of skill (judged answers, genuine skill tests) is treated differently from a lottery or game of chance, which is state-regulated and mostly restricted. If entry requires purchase and winning is pure chance, you are drifting toward an illegal lottery. Structure contests around skill, keep a free entry route where required, and publish winner selection methods.

Wow Widget: The ASCI Complaint Walkthrough

The Advertising Standards Council of India is self-regulation with real teeth: broadcasters and platforms honour its rulings. Tap each step to see how a misleading promotional claim travels from complaint to consequence.

1

Anyone can complain: a consumer via ASCI's website or WhatsApp line, a rival brand, a government body, or ASCI's own monitoring, which actively scans ads. No lawyer, no fee for consumers. The complaint cites the ad and why it seems to breach the Code: misleading, indecent, harmful, or unfair to competition.

2

ASCI's secretariat checks jurisdiction and prima facie merit: is this an advertisement, does the objection map to a Code provision? Frivolous or out-of-scope complaints are filtered here, and clear-cut cases can move on a fast track.

3

The advertiser gets the complaint and a deadline to substantiate the claim with evidence: test reports, study data, terms of the offer. This is the moment your file either exists or does not. If the claim was "India's No. 1", the ranking study must be produced, not remembered.

4

The Consumer Complaints Council, a jury of industry professionals and civil society members, weighs the ad against the Code. Deliberation is structured: does the evidence substantiate the claim as an ordinary consumer would understand it? The ordinary-consumer reading, not the lawyer's reading, decides.

5

If upheld, the advertiser must modify or withdraw the ad by a set date. Non-compliance gets escalated: broadcasters can refuse the spot, and matters can be forwarded to regulators like the CCPA. Rulings are published, so the reputational cost is real.

Real pattern, anonymised: a snack brand's pack and posts claimed "No added preservatives" while the ingredient list included a preservative-class additive; the complaint was upheld and packs plus posts were ordered corrected. In another upheld case, a learning app's promotion promised guaranteed rank improvement with no substantiating study; "guaranteed" had to go. Notice the common thread: the claim was written by enthusiasm, not evidence.

Discussion prompt for your team: pull up your current campaign. Which single claim would survive step 3 least comfortably, and what evidence file should exist for it today?

The communicator's responsibility: your refusal list

Regulation defines what you cannot do. Character defines what you will not do. Somewhere between an inflated claim and an outright lie sits every grey brief you will ever receive. Write, right now, the three things you would refuse to sign off on, whatever the client, whatever the quarter. Keep the list where you can see it, because the day you need it, there will be a deadline attached.

This stays on your screen. It is for you, not for submission. That is rather the point.
