Your two weakest zones

Audience

Why "everyone aged 18 to 45" is not an audience

An audience you cannot picture is an audience you cannot move. "Everyone aged 18 to 45" describes roughly 600 million Indians: a college student in Patna, a fund manager in Bandra, a farmer's son scrolling Reels in Nashik. No single message respects all three, so the campaign respects none of them.

Age band, city tier, income, occupation, life stage. Necessary but never sufficient. Two women, both 32, both in Pune, both earning 12 lakh: one is repaying a home loan, the other is funding a startup. Demographics put them in the same box. Your campaign cannot.
Values, aspirations, anxieties, identity. This is where Indian marketing gets interesting: the Tier 2 buyer often out-aspires the metro buyer. When Lenskart priced designer-style eyewear within a college student's budget, it was reading psychographics, not census tables.
What they actually watch, buy, share, and abandon. Media habits, purchase triggers, category rituals. Behaviour is the only layer that never flatters itself. When stated attitude and observed behaviour disagree, plan on the behaviour.

Indian Case

Tata Tea "Jaago Re": objective-led planning

Jaago Re never sold tea leaves. It set a communication objective, waking a socially passive audience, and let the brand ride the awakening. Each wave had a defined objective: voter registration in one, anti-corruption in another, pre-activism in 2017. Objective first, creative second. That sequencing is the lesson.

The campaign converted a commodity ritual, morning tea, into a moment of civic conscience. Because the objective was defined as attitudinal shift rather than sales lift, the KPIs tracked conversation, registrations, and brand association scores. Sales followed as a lagging indicator. When you present your plan, be ready to say which of your KPIs are leading and which are lagging.
Indian Case

Swachh Bharat: layered objectives at national scale

A social cause campaign with objectives stacked in layers: awareness of sanitation, attitude shift on open defecation, behaviour change in toilet construction and use. Each layer had different messengers, from film stars to sarpanches, and different metrics. One campaign, many funnels running in parallel.

The planning insight: behaviour change objectives need years and repetition, while awareness objectives need weeks and reach. Swachh Bharat budgeted and scheduled these separately. If your campaign plan gives one timeline to objectives that mature at different speeds, expect that question in the room.
Indian Case · D2C

The D2C funnel discipline: Mamaearth and Lenskart

Watch how disciplined D2C brands assign objectives per funnel stage. Mamaearth used influencer-led content for awareness among young parents, toxin-free proof points for consideration, and first-order discounts for trial. Lenskart used celebrity fame and Shark Tank visibility for awareness, the 3D try-on and home eye check-up for consideration, and first-frame offers for trial. Neither brand asked one ad to do all four jobs. That restraint is a planning decision, not a creative one.

Block 2 · Budgeting and Channel Mix

Money follows objectives, or money gets wasted

The budget conversation is where marketing earns or loses its seat at the table. This block trains you in zero-based logic: start from zero, justify every rupee against a task, and allocate channels by objective and audience, not by habit or by whoever shouted loudest last year.

The Core Argument

Zero-based versus "last year plus 10 percent"

"Last year plus 10 percent" feels safe and takes ten minutes. It is also how brands keep funding channels their audience left two years ago. Zero-based budgeting asks a harder question: if this campaign began today with nothing, what tasks must be done, and what does each task honestly cost? The total emerges from the tasks. It is the objective-and-task method, and it is the only budget you can defend line by line when the CFO leans forward.

Budget methods grounding: Belch and Belch, objective-and-task method; Kotler and Keller on top-down versus build-up budgeting

What "last year plus 10 percent" hides from you:

Last year's number contains last year's mistakes, compounded annually. The print spend nobody questioned in 2022 is still in there, now 40 percent larger, still reaching nobody under 50.
A lump sum never asks "what must be done?" So new tasks, a WhatsApp CRM build, a creator programme, quietly get funded from contingency, which means they get funded badly.
When finance cuts a zero-based budget, they must name the task they are killing. When they cut a lump, they just cut 15 percent, and you absorb it everywhere, which means everywhere gets weaker.
Integration Over Amplification

One anchor idea echoed everywhere beats one loud channel

Recall the creative anchor you built in Unit II. Channel selection is not about being everywhere. It is about the same anchor idea arriving through every door your audience opens. A campaign shouting on TV while whispering something different on Instagram is not integrated, it is just expensive.

Before approving any channel, ask three questions. One: can this channel carry the anchor message without distorting it? Two: does my audience actually live here, per the persona card, not per habit? Three: does this channel hand the audience to the next funnel stage, or is it a dead end? If a channel fails two of three, the money belongs elsewhere.
Reach multiplies exposure, integration multiplies memory. Ten exposures to ten different messages build ten weak traces. Ten exposures to one anchor idea in ten forms build one deep groove. Media money buys the exposures; only planning discipline buys the groove.

Block 3 · Execution and Coordination

Where great plans go to get tested

Execution is a contact sport played by six teams who report to different bosses. The creative brief is your single source of truth, the timeline is your physics, and coordination is the craft nobody teaches but everybody gets judged on during launch week.

The Anchor Document

The creative brief: one page every team executes against

Everything you built in Unit II about the creative anchor becomes operational here. The brief is not paperwork, it is the contract between strategy and execution. Tap through the checklist any launch-worthy brief must survive.

One: the business problem in one sentence. Two: the single audience, by persona, not demography. Three: the one thing we want them to think or do. Four: the anchor idea from Unit II, stated in under 15 words. Five: mandatories and constraints, including budget class and ASCI compliance. Six: how we will know it worked, with the KPI named. If any line needs a paragraph, the thinking is not finished.
A real pattern you will recognise: the brief says "young, digital-first audience, make it viral, premium but accessible." Creative interprets premium. Media interprets digital-first. The client meant Tier 2 value seekers. Three weeks later the agency presents a film the client calls "beautiful but not us," and the launch slips past the season it was built for. The brief did not fail loudly on day one. It failed expensively on day twenty. Vagueness always invoices you later.
Brief discipline grounding: Batra, Myers and Aaker on advertising management

Indian Rhythm

Teaser, launch, sustain: the festive and IPL cadence

Indian launch calendars breathe with cricket and festivals. A Diwali campaign begins its teaser in Navratri, bursts in the Dhanteras window, and sustains through the New Year gifting tail. An IPL-season launch teases before the opening match, bursts around marquee fixtures, and sustains with reactive social through the playoffs. The rhythm is teaser to build curiosity, burst to buy dominance in a short window, sustain to convert the interested. Brands that spend the burst budget evenly across ten weeks buy ten weeks of being ignorable.

Field Activity 04

The Honest Post-Mortem

Take one brand you have worked on, or one you know intimately. Write its last campaign's post-mortem in the honest format, the one that gets pinned to the wall, not buried in a drive.

  • What we planned: objectives and targets as they were actually written.
  • What happened: results without adjectives.
  • What we learned: including what we got right by accident.
  • What we will never repeat: named specifically enough to be actionable.
  • If data access is limited, use public signals: views, comment sentiment, follower shifts, and state your confidence level per claim.
Deliverable: one-page post-mortem · 1 week
Block 5 · Campaign Resilience and Contingency

The plan behind the plan

Everything you learned about crisis foundations in Unit IV becomes muscle here. Campaigns do not fail because teams lacked talent. They fail because nobody asked "what if the celebrity tweets something at midnight" until the celebrity tweeted something at midnight. Resilience is a planning line item, not a personality trait.

The Discipline

Critical versus flexible: know the difference before the fire

Every campaign component is either critical, meaning launch cannot happen without it, or flexible, meaning launch limps but lives. Teams that cannot tell the difference burn their contingency budget on the wrong fires.

Launch week. The event backdrop arrives in the wrong Pantone, and the hero film's final grade is stuck with a vendor. A panicked team spends its buffer reprinting backdrops overnight. The backdrop was flexible: guests forgive a colour. The film was critical: the media buy airing in 48 hours cannot run without it. Contingency spent on cosmetics is contingency stolen from survival.
A festive campaign's courier vendor collapses in week one, threatening influencer kit deliveries. The lead had pre-rated vendors by failure probability and had Backup A contracted at a 5 percent premium. Kits shipped 36 hours late instead of ten days late. Nobody outside the team ever knew. That invisibility is what excellent contingency looks like.

The One-Pager

A crisis document any team member can act on

Linked straight back to Unit IV's crisis foundations: the document is useless if only its author understands it. Tap each section of the specimen.

Who declares the crisis, on which channel, using which exact phrase. One named decision-maker with one named alternate. All external communication freezes until the declaration. The intern who spots it at 1 am must know exactly whom to wake.
Three pre-approved sentences: one for press, one for social, one for internal teams. Written in calm daylight, legal-cleared, stored where everyone can find them. Crisis-hour writing is always worse than crisis-eve writing.
The pre-agreed triggers: at what point does Backup A activate, who spends the contingency without further approval, and which media bookings pause automatically. Rules decided in advance turn panic into procedure.
Indian Case

Rain over the activation

IPL-season outdoor activations and fan zones live under Indian skies. The professional plan prices the weather: indoor fallback venues, waterproofed staging, and insurance riders. The amateur plan prays. Monsoon is not a black swan, it is a calendar entry.

Indian Case

The endorser at midnight

A celebrity controversy breaks after prime time, and by morning the brand's face is the story. Brands with resilience plans had non-celebrity edits of every asset ready, contractual morality clauses, and a 12-hour swap protocol. The overnight creative swap is only possible if it was edited weeks earlier.

Diwali-season fulfilment is the most predictable stress test in Indian marketing, and it still catches brands every year. Pre-rated backup vendors, split shipments, and honest lead-time buffers separate the brands that trend for delight from the ones that trend for apology.

Field Activity 05

The Live Event Risk Hunt

Attend or observe any live event: a college fest, an exhibition, a product launch, even a wedding-scale event. Every one of them is a contingency masterclass in progress.

  • Identify the three highest-risk components you can observe: power, weather exposure, crowd flow, single points of failure.
  • For each, infer what backup existed, or visibly did not.
  • If possible, interview one event manager: what was your worst on-ground failure, and what saved you?
  • Write a half-page risk map: component, probability, priority, observed backup.
Deliverable: half-page risk map · 2 weeks
Block 6 · Sustainability in IMC

The footprint your campaign leaves behind

Clients, boards, and regulators now ask a question that did not exist in older textbooks: what did this campaign cost the world, beyond the media plan? Sustainability assessment is becoming a standard planning gate, and the greenwashing trap, where the claim outruns the operations, connects straight back to Unit IV's authenticity litmus.

Indian Examples

The shift already underway

Indian brands are moving festive campaigns digital-first, cutting print runs and event waste. Packaging tie-ins now carry the campaign into the product: refill packs, plantable packaging, take-back programmes. The campaign and the operations are becoming one claim, which is exactly how it should be.

The Trap

Greenwashing: when the claim outruns the operations

Unit IV's authenticity litmus applies with full force. If the film shows forests but the supply chain shows otherwise, the campaign has manufactured its own crisis, and journalists, regulators, and ASCI guidelines on environmental claims are all watching. The rule is simple: claim only what the operations can survive an audit on.

Presentation Checklist

Before your team walks into the room

  • Time limit: 12 minutes for the plan, 5 for questions. Rehearse to 11.
  • Who presents what: strategy opens, budget owner defends the numbers, resilience owner takes the "what if" round.
  • Likely jury question 1: "Why this budget and not half of it?" Answer from tasks, never from last year.
  • Likely jury question 2: "Which single metric proves the campaign worked?" Name a branch or trunk metric, never a leaf.
  • Likely jury question 3: "What breaks first, and what is Backup A?" Answer within ten seconds or the plan looks fragile.
